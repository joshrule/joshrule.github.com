Josh Rule · US residents are extremely materially wealthy

· GitHub · Scholar

The average US resident is extremely materially wealthy, both globally and historically.

We are materially wealthy globally speaking.

As of the writing of this article, Elon Musk is currently the world’s richest individual, with over $450B. Perhaps you know that name and have opinions about how he ought to use that money. Not many, however, know Serrin Dossa. Bill Gates tells some of her story: she sells corn porridge in Lagos for less than $2 a day to support herself, her husband, and three kids.

Few of us face these financial extremes, but where exactly do you think you sit between Elon and Serrin?

Say you earn the federal minimum wage of $7.25/hr for 20hrs a week. Working 50 weeks a year, you’d earn just $7,250. Even then, Giving What We Can estimates that you could afford a better quality of life than 72 of every 100 people on the planet. With the US median per capita income of $43,289, that number jumps to 97 out of 100 people, and at just $62,883 each year, you become part of the global 1%. Perhaps these comparisons surprise you; I know I consistently underestimate how materially wealthy the US is. Either way, the point remains that most US residents are, globally speaking, extremely wealthy.

We are also materially wealthy historically speaking.

Eric Beinhocker estimates in The Origin of Wealth that the residents of the ancient Mediterranean—including the original audience for Jesus’ teaching—earned (adjusting for inflation) approximately $240 per person per year or $0.65 per day. That would put them in extreme poverty, a far cry from our modern wealth. Even just 200 years ago, roughly 80% of the planet lived in these conditions (today, it’s about 10%, and still falling).

Also, let’s not forget that even the most privileged elite of days gone by—including kings, queens, emperors, and popes—didn’t have access to the Internet, consumer electronics, modern medicine, or even modern plumbing. Now, there’s nuance to this argument, which is perhaps one reason internet opinions are divided about whether historical kings and queens had better lives than the average US resident today (e.g. here, here, here, and here). Royalty had staff do most of their work for them, and they often had fiefdoms and empires to provide other resources. Even so, no staff could replace modern medicine or access to information. On balance, the point stands that most US residents are, historically speaking, extremely wealthy.
