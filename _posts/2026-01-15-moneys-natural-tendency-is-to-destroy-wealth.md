title: "Money's natural tendency is to destroy wealth"
---

Rather than being a wealth builder, money, left to its own devices, is
actually a wealth destroyer.

On the face of it, that claim might seem impossible, because so many of
the messages about wealth that we encounter today identify money as a
kind of wealth. More strongly, the most typical story is perhaps that
money is what defines wealth, that other things make you wealthy in so
far as they can be translated into money.

The biblical perspective, however, is that [real wealth lies in right
relationship]({% post_url 2026-01-15-true-wealth-lies-in-godly-relationships %})
with God and all that he has made. Real wealth lies in a life of shalom.

Now, it is incredibly difficult to use money to build God-honoring right
relationships, because the natural tendency of money is to destroy godly
relationships rather than to build them. If true wealth lies in godly
relationships, that means that the natural tendency of money is to
destroy wealth rather than to build it.

Author and thinker [Andy
Crouch](https://faithdriveninvestor.org/episode-92-god-mammon-with-andy-crouch/)
does a great job of explaining this idea that it's difficult to use
money to build godly relationships. He focuses on Matthew 6:24, where
Jesus says, "No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the
one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the
other. You cannot serve God and money."

Now, the main point is clear. You can serve God or you can serve money.
But we can't serve both because when push comes to shove, we will
inevitably choose one over the other, revealing our true priorities.

But, there's more here. If we look more closely at this passage, we find
that in the original Greek, Jesus didn't say "You cannot serve God and
money." He said, "You cannot serve God and mammon." And mammon is not
the Greek word for money. It's not even a Greek word at all.

My current understanding is that mammon is instead an Aramaic word. Its exact
history is unclear; no equivalent word shows up in Old Testament Hebrew, but it
appears four times in the New Testament, in the Qumran documents (i.e. the Dead
Sea Scrolls) and in later Aramaic and Hebrew texts. Some suggest that
Mammon is actually the name of a false god from the surrounding Canaanite
cultures, the personification of money and the god of greed. In that case, it
would clearly be a reference to wealth. Others, including [Tim Mackie at the
Bible Project](https://bibleproject.com/classroom/messianic-torah/sessions/15),
suggest "mamona" may be a nominalization of the word "amen". The two words
apparently share the same root consonants in Aramaic, and the form of mamona
makes it a noun. Because "amen" means something like "trustworthy", "mamona"
would then mean something like "the thing in which one trusts". Given that the
surrounding passages are all about wealth, the contrast here would be between
trusting God or trusting something else, namely wealth.

To see why even attempting to serve God and money is a contradiction in terms,
observe that money is fungible, storable, countable, impersonal power. Every
dollar wields the same small amount of power that can be turned into other
things. It's fungible. It's storable and countable. I can put money in a bank
account, look at my balance, and know exactly how much I have. Finally, money is
impersonal. It doesn't rely on relationships, charisma, or position. If I have
money, it doesn't matter who I am. I can get things done. I can bend others to
my will. Instead of building loving relationships, money makes it easy for us to
simply transactionalize relationship. Do what I want because I have the dollars
to make you want to do it. So, if we use money without thinking, or make the
spending of money the primary model we have for our interactions in the world,
we cut the legs out from under the vision of life God invites us into, a world
of loving relationship, a world of shalom.

And, this is why we simply cannot serve God and money. They provide
totally different pictures about what relationships are and how they
operate. One is based on the genuine understanding and care required of
real love, and the other is based on impersonal, transactional power.
And, that is why misunderstanding wealth and treating money and
possessions as wealth can destroy peace rather than lead us to peace,
destroying wealth rather than building it.
