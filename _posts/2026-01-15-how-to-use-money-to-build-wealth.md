title: "How to use money to build wealth"
---

True wealth lies in cultivating right relationships with God and with
all that he has made. While we often treat money as a store of wealth,
money's natural tendency---because it is a way to store and wield
impersonal power---is actually to erode God-honoring relationships
rather than to build them.

One response to the revelation that money naturally erodes wealth is to
abandon money altogether. That is, if money's default is to hinder
wealth building, then perhaps the best thing we can do is write off
money as a failed experiment. Even if money showed some promise over its
millennia-long run, maybe we'd be better off building a new economy on a
different foundation, one which encourages godly relationships by
default rather than discouraging them. Certainly, it is possible to live
life in the modern western world without money. [Daniel
Suelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suelo), [Mark
Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Boyle_(Moneyless_Man)), and a
handful of others have done it for years at a time, and there are
moments when I personally find the idea attractive. While it goes well
beyond the scope of this article, pursuing a life built around
cultivating godly relationships might eventually lead us to a world
where money simply is necessary. It's certainly not something to dismiss
out of hand.

For most, however, it is likely to be more fruitful over the
short-and-medium-term to consider whether we can defy money's natural
tendencies and use it in ways that actually do help us build godly
relationships.

The good news is that, yes, we can. In fact, the process is pretty
simple. Using money to build true wealth boils down to choosing uses
that encourage every created thing to be in right relationship with God
and with one another.

Like I said, this principle is pretty simple---say yes to those uses
that best encourage godly relationships, and say no to the rest---but it
isn't necessarily easy. It might mean limiting spending so that you can
give more to the local church or to the vulnerable. Could we give up
Netflix if we knew that by giving that money away, [we would save a
life](https://www.givewell.org/how-much-does-it-cost-to-save-a-life)
(The \$3000 cost reported here is the cost to "save a life", a term that
is typically defined in this domain as extending a person's
life-expectancy by 30yrs. If so, then it only costs \~\$100 to add an
entire year to the life expectancy of the most vulnerable. Currently,
Netflix's Standard subscription is \$17.99/mo or \$215.88/yr. By giving
up Netflix, you can give two people another year of life!)? On the other
hand, it might mean spending more on certain items, choosing to spend
money on goods and services which we know are produced in ways that help
people and planet rather than exploiting and/or harming them. Do we
really need another 8-pack of \$3 t-shirts sewn by people earning poor
wages out of fabrics whose production harms the local ecosystem? Perhaps
we could get by with fewer shirts that cost more individually because
those very same people are being paid a fair wage, and the suppliers are
being compensated well enough that they can afford to produce textiles
in ways that nourish the land rather than harming it. Better yet, maybe
we could buy from a thrift shop or just use the shirts we already have.

Navigating these kinds of decisions across our entire financial life
won't be easy. It will require patient investigation and careful
consideration of our options. We won't always succeed. Continued
practice, however, will make it increasingly natural to use money in
ways that build wealth rather than erode it.
