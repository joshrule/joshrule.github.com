Josh Rule · How wealth works: Pursue right relationship rather than money

· GitHub · Scholar

Many of us have been taught to think about wealth in a way that completely contradicts reality.

The prevailing western view is that wealth lies in having enough resources, primarily money, to achieve our goals and satisfy our desires. With this view, accumulating money—whether through steady work, successful investing, or entrepreneurial hustling—is the primary way we accumulate wealth. Some counter that real wealth lies instead in having time, attention, or experiences, rather than money, but this counterpoint is really just part of the prevailing view. Even for those who identify money as wealth, money is rarely the ultimate goal or desire. It is instead to live a life of leisure, have freedom over our attention, or enjoy interesting experiences.

Jesus, however, sees wealth differently and distinguishes wealth in heaven from wealth on earth. In Matthew 6:19–20, he says, “Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys, and where thieves do not break in or steal.” So, not all wealth is of a kind. Wealth on earth is distinct from wealth in heaven.

To be clear, Jesus isn’t saying here that this earthly life is completely worthless or that only what we have access to after we die is of value. When Jesus talks about “earth” here, he’s talking about life apart from God. When he talks about “heaven”, he’s talking about life with God. So, in distinguishing treasure on earth from treasure in heaven, he’s distinguishing what we see as valuable in a life apart from God from what we see as valuable in life with God.

When Jesus talks about treasures on earth, he seems to be focused on money and possessions, things that we can “store up” for ourselves. The main thing he says about these things is that they provide no long-term value. They will be stolen, whether by a burglar or by time itself. Jesus isn’t alone in making this claim. When we look across the Bible, we consistently find that money and possessions have no long-term value (e.g. Ecclesiastes 2:4–1,5:15; 1 Timothy 6:7; Proverbs 11:4; Matthew 16:26; 1 Peter 1:7,18; Proverbs 23:4–5). They aren’t real wealth.

By contrast, Jesus talks about treasure in heaven as having long-term value. Whatever treasure in heaven is, it never fades and cannot be stolen. Again, when we look across the Bible, it becomes clear that treasure in heaven lies not in money or possessions, but in right relationship with God and all that he has made (Philippians 3:7–8; 1 Peter 1:7; Job 28; Psalm 119:72; Proverbs 22:1; Luke 12:33–34; Genesis 1:28; Matthew 22:37–40). These relational ties are our true wealth.

From Jesus’ perspective, rooting wealth in money and possessions is completely backward. In fact, as we continue to meditate on the idea that wealth lies in godly relationships, we’ll see that not only that money is not wealth, but it is, when used according to its natural tendencies, actually a wealth destroyer rather than a wealth builder.

If money is naturally a wealth destroyer, we do well to take great care in using it so that it becomes an aid in wealth building rather than an obstacle or hindrance. Indeed, the right way to use all our resources, including money, is to use them as tools for cultivating our relationship with God and, from that, our relationships with all that God has made.
